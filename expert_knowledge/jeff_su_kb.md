### Core Thinking Models

Apply to ANY behavioral / "tell me about a time" interview question (biggest weakness, conflict, difficult situation). He argues Action and Results are the load-bearing pillars; Context and Learnings play a complementary role.
- **The 6-Component Prompt Formula** (Task, Context, Exemplar, Persona, Format, Tone) — his "only formula you'll ever need" for prompting AI. Apply whenever using ChatGPT/Gemini/Claude for interview prep, resume bullets, cover letters, or work tasks.

- **The Business Case Framework / MECE thinking** (Victor Cheng's Customer, Product, Company, Competition) — apply to case interviews, ramping into a new team, annual planning, or any overwhelming problem: (1) break the problem into parts, (2) gather info per part by asking questions, (3) summarize with an actionable recommendation. Pairs with **BLUF** (Bottom Line Up Front) and **MECE** (Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive — "coined by McKinsey").
- **The 3-Step Unpreparable-Question Process** (Stall for time → Break it into manageable segments → Answer each segment) — apply to curveball interview questions you couldn't have prepared for. The point is a clear, easy-to-follow *thought process*, not a "correct" answer.
- **Google's 4 Interview Attributes** (General Cognitive Ability / GCA, Role-Related Knowledge / RRK, Googliness, Leadership) — the mental model for what non-technical big-tech interviewers actually score. Apply when preparing for structured big-tech interviews; prep GCA using consulting case questions.
- **Capture → Organize** (his simplified 2-step take on Tiago Forte's CODE / David Allen's GTD) — apply to notes, follow-ups, and job-search admin: "organize by the location you will use the information," not where you found it.

### Anti-Patterns (NEVER do these)
- Never claim a weakness that's "really a strength in disguise" — "Nope. It's not okay to say a weakness that's really a strength in disguise." And never name a **deal-breaker** weakness (can't manage multiple projects, not detail-oriented, not a people person).
- Never present a "perfect fairy tale ending" to a conflict/difficult-situation question — a flawless resolution "seems a little too good to be true" and cracks under follow-up. A less-than-ideal outcome is more convincing.
- Never give vague, generic, "subjective" advice ("what works for me might not work for you"). Ground claims in data, Google's official guidance, or named experts. Never fluffy buzzwords with no impact ("assisted with project governance," "presented to a banking client") — show the *result*.
- Never write resume bullets without metrics, and never skip quantifying because your numbers seem unimpressive — "recruiters and hiring managers care much more about the effort you put into quantifying your impact rather than the actual numbers themselves." Never send the same untailored resume to 50 jobs.
- Never send a generic networking message ("can I get 30 minutes to pick your brain") — always end an outreach with a request to be connected to someone in the actual role.
- Never over-format or bury the lede in a recap/email — "All the formalities and formation in the world are irrelevant if no one reads past the first sentence." Always lead with a TL;DR of quantifiable results.
- Never waste the interviewee's/reader's time: no long-winded answers, no info dumps, no reading the whole framework aloud when a checklist will do.

## Detailed Frameworks & Playbooks

### 1. CARL — the behavioral answer structure ("star on steroids")
**Steps:** Context → Action → Results → Learnings. He has "done a whole video on why CARL is a superior answer structure to the STAR method."
- **Context:** name a conflict/situation realistically relevant to the role you're applying for. Verbatim guidance: "if you're applying for a job in a technology company where the team structures are relatively flat, a meaningful conflict is how you tried to influence others without authority and the challenges that you faced." Context plays a complementary role — keep it tight.
- **Action (pillar #1):** the meat of a conflict answer. Show two things: "number one, you are able to listen to and accept other people's point of views even if you have already formed your own opinion and number two, you're able to put the needs of the business above your own." Tactically: ask the other party to expand on *why* they believe they're correct, then "paraphrase the answer, and repeating it back to them" to prove you put yourself in their shoes.
- **Results (pillar #2):** "pinpoint the exact moment where all parties involved are at the most risk of losing face," then de-escalate "by providing a platform for everyone to step down on" (suggest a cool-off period; acknowledge the other idea may have more merit). Show how "the broader team or business benefited." A less-than-ideal outcome is fine — "any outcome you end your answer with is simply a by-product of how well you did those two things."
- **Learnings:** "recap what you did, explain why it was a correct course of action in that instance and what you would do in a similar situation next time."

Exact quotes: "a good old buddy CARL, context, action, results and learnings." / On the biggest-weakness question: "you should use the CARL answer format, which is star on steroids." / On conflict, apply Nelson Mandela: "Be the last one to speak" — "letting the other person feel heard, is half the battle even if that person's ideas are rejected in the end."

**Difficult-situation / conflict question, core principle:** the interviewer "is not looking for a perfect ending." Don't fall into "the trap of thinking a flawless conflict resolution is all that's required to pass this question" — "very rarely are workplace conflicts so cleanly resolved," and the facade cracks on follow-up questions.

**Biggest-weakness question — 3 tips + full sample:**
1. **Stay away from deal-breakers.** "there are such things as wrong answers." Deal-breakers = "not being able to manage multiple projects at once, not being detail oriented and not being a people person." Avoid them because "hiring managers generally expect a basic level of competency."
2. **Highlight a skill expected of someone more senior** — "a skill you currently lack, but you see in someone in a more senior position." Safe because "at your current level, you're not expected to have that particular skill anyways." Example: a junior person naming *strategic thinking* as a weakness.
3. **Give a concrete example** — "purposely shining a light on a mistake" shows strong self-awareness AND lets you "sneak in an explanation on what steps you're taking to improve." Structure the example with CARL.
- Verbatim sample: *"my biggest weakness is that sometimes I lack the confidence to present my work, especially in a larger group setting."* Story: at a QBR his manager was pulled away last-minute; with two days' notice he practiced/memorized and "emailed my slides to the other teams beforehand as a preread" so he'd field fewer live questions. Closes honestly: "While I can't say I'm suddenly a confident speaker, I think aligning with team members ahead of a meeting like that is a great way to avoid surprises."

### 2. The 3-Step Process for Unpreparable Interview Questions
Real question he got: *"If I spoke with 10 random people in your life right now, how many of them would dislike you?"* — "the main takeaway... should really be the process I used to answer a question I didn't prepare for."
- **Step 1 — Stall for time (3 sub-moves).** Misconception: buying time looks unprepared. "In fact, it's quite the opposite."
  - (a) **Ask clarifying questions** — "would these people be my family, friends, or colleagues? Do we assume they all have to give an honest answer? When was the last time they interacted with me?" This "showcas[es] problem solving skills by conveying to the interviewer that I'm trying to gather as much relevant information as possible."
  - (b) **Rephrase and repeat the question back** — "just to make sure I understand the question correctly, you want to know out of my entire life, if you picked 10 people at random..." Buys "at least two minutes."
  - (c) **Ask for a minute or two to structure your thoughts on pen and paper** — "the interviewer will always give you time to structure your answer," and hearing a prepared answer "gives them a much more accurate sense of how you perform on a day to day basis."
- **Step 2 — Break the question into manageable segments** using MECE. Make assumptions when little info is given: he assumed 5 from personal life + 5 from professional life with no overlap, "mainly because number one, I knew the math would turn out easy, five and five. And number two, using the professional and personal groupings is a mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive, MECE way to break down my relationships. The MECE Principle was coined by McKinsey." Then sub-segment (e.g., of 5 personal: 4 friends + 1 family).
- **Step 3 — Answer each segment out loud.** Walk each bucket ("they're my family, hopefully... zero out of one") and total it. "as I was making those assumptions and segmenting the groups, I was articulating all of that out loud. So the interviewer could easily follow my train of thought." He passed even though "my answer was not the one she was looking for... she could see I had a very clear thought process." Key lesson: "she didn't give me a number out of 10 as a correct answer... It was more about the logic I used to arrive at my answer."

### 3. The Business Case Framework (Victor Cheng) + BLUF for case interviews
Example: *"Profits for Noiceflix is down 20% year on year. Why?"*
- Break the problem into parts: Profits = Revenue − Costs.
- Drill each part with relevant questions ("what are the top three revenue and cost drivers in the past year?").
- Summarize with **BLUF (Bottom Line Up Front)**: "The 20% year-on-year decrease in profits is driven by an over-hiring in the domestic streaming department... We know this because of A, B, and C."
- The four-part version (Customer, Product, Company, Competition) applies far beyond interviews — ramping into a new team, annual planning, even starting a YouTube channel. "there is no correct answer but it helps me organize my thoughts in a very structured way."
- Recommended prep books: Victor Cheng's *Case Interview Secrets*; *Case in Point*.

### 4. Google's Non-Technical Interview Attributes
Four attributes interviewers score: **GCA** (General Cognitive Ability), **RRK** (Role-Related Knowledge), **Googliness**, **Leadership.** "GCA is where I think most people get tripped up" — prep it with consulting case questions because "the approach to both is very similar." Two more of his day-in-the-life tips: use the **XYZ** bullet structure on resumes (Google's own guidance); use **Google's Interview Warm-up tool** (or role-play with Gemini as the interviewer) to build confidence with industry-specific questions.

### 5. The 6-Component Prompt Formula (for AI-assisted prep)
Task, Context, Exemplar, Persona, Format, Tone — "the only formula you will ever need to master prompting." Two rules: know the components, AND know their order of importance.
- **Order of importance:** Task is *mandatory* ("if we just give ChatGPT the context nothing really happens"); Context + Exemplar are *important*; Persona, Format, Tone are *nice to have*. Go down the checklist so you "include just enough relevant information."
- **Task:** "always start the task sentence with an action verb — generate, give, write, analyze — and clearly articulate what your end goal is." Can be one step or a chained three-step ask.
- **Context:** ask three questions — "first, what's the user's background? second, what does success look like? and third, what environment are they in?" Give "just enough information to constrain the endless possibilities."
- **Exemplar** (just a fancy word for examples): "including examples within the prompt drastically improves the quality of the output." A *framework* counts as an example structure — "use the STAR answer framework, situation task action and results," or the XYZ resume structure.
- **Persona:** "think of someone you wish you had instant access to with the task you're facing." Job seeker → "a recruiter or hiring manager." Named individuals work "only when they're famous enough like Warren Buffett, Steve Jobs, Jeff Su."
- **Format:** "literally close your eyes and visualize how exactly you want the end result to look" (table, email, bullet points, markdown, code block). Pro tip when proofreading: "specify that all changes need to be bolded."
- **Tone:** "use a casual or formal tone of voice." Since we can't recall adjectives on demand, "tell ChatGPT the feeling you're going for" or ask it for "a list of five tone keywords."
- Interview-prep application (GPT-5 video): "act as a hiring manager and based on my resume and job description, ask me three questions I'm likely to face," structured as an **XML sandwich** (`<task>…</task>`, `<resume>…</resume>`, `<job description>…</job description>`).

### 6. Chain-of-Thought for cover letters & resumes
"when you divide a single task into more manageable steps you help the large language model produce accurate and consistent results." For a cover letter: Step 1 "based on my resume and the job description write an attention-grabbing hook," tweak it, then Step 2 paste it back and ask for the body paragraph, repeat for the closing — instead of asking for a whole cover letter in one shot. Also "surface the implied context" (like telling a restaurant it's for a vegetarian friend) so outputs aren't generic — worked example: when negotiating a raise, tell the AI last year's 10% increase, that you're the top performer, and the 12% industry average, so it doesn't give "a lower quality AKA more generic output." Know **zero-shot / one-shot / few-shot** ("the word shot simply means examples") — more relevant examples = more relevant output.

### 8. GPT-5-era prompting upgrades (useful for high-stakes prep)
Five tips (easy → hard): (1) **router nudge phrases** — end a prompt with "think hard about this" / "think deeply about this" / "think carefully" to force a higher-reasoning model on high-stakes tasks; (2) **verbosity control** — "give me the bottom line in 100 words or less," "aim for a concise 3 to five paragraph explanation," or "provide a comprehensive and detailed breakdown, 600 to 800 words"; (3) **prompt optimizer / meta-prompt** — "You are an expert prompt engineer... take my prompt and make it better"; (4) **XML sandwich** — wrap components in tags (`<task>`, `<resume>`, `<job description>`) because GPT-5 follows instructions literally; (5) **the perfection loop** — "develop an internal rubric... iterate and refine the draft until it scores top marks against your rubric." Interview application: `<task>act as a hiring manager and based on my resume and job description, ask me three questions I'm likely to face</task>`. Also: default paid models to *thinking*, not *auto* — "the intelligent routing function does not work well."

### 7. Capture → Organize (personal productivity that powers the job search)
Two steps: **Capture** every idea/task frictionlessly (he uses a Todoist widget, "start your quick capture with an action verb"), then **Organize** into the location where you'll *use* the info (Tiago Forte: "The wrong way to organize information is by where you found it, the right way is to organize by the location you will use that information"). Directly applied to follow-ups: write the post-coffee-chat thank-you email at 5 PM when it's fresh, but **Schedule Send** it for 9:30 the next morning.

## Signature Playbook

### Resume bullet formula (XYZ + "so what?")
- **Formula (Google's own):** "Accomplished [X] as measured by [Y] by doing [Z]." Cited example: instead of "I designed a new onboarding process to increase customer satisfaction," write "Increased customer satisfaction [X] by 22% in 3 months [as measured by Y] through designing a new onboarding process [by doing Z]." "apply this XYZ framework to every single bullet point on your resume." Alt phrasing he uses for AI rewrites: "I accomplished X by the measure Y that resulted in Z" (e.g., "I lowered hospital mortality rate by 10% by educating nurses in new protocols which translates to 200 lives saved per year").
- **The "so what?" ladder** to force metrics — "keep asking yourself, so what?":
  - "Taught SAT during my summer break in college." → so what?
  - "The average increase in my students' SAT scores was 280 points." → so what?
  - "Seven out of the eight seniors got accepted into their first choice colleges." → impact.
- **Metrics when yours seem unimpressive — benchmark:** his real first-internship bullet "my presentation scored 23% higher than the class average" is strong because "it shows I knew the success metric of the presentation, the score, and I took the extra step to benchmark my performance to that of my peers." Extreme illustration: "delivered on average nine coffees a day with 100% accuracy on all orders for three months" beats "I fetched coffee for senior associates during my summer internship."
- **5 resume mistakes:**
  1. **Education above work experience** — recruiters know "any sort of work experience... translates much better to on the job performance." A hiring manager who sees GPA/classes/honor roll first "just wasted 30 seconds of her life, and she moves on."
  2. **Not showing impact** — fluffy overcomplicated words ("assisted with project governance, presented to a banking client") with no implication. Show the result: "the banking client successfully opened a branch in the local market." (Backed by Austin Belcak, Adam Broda, and even Ali Abdaal.)
  3. **Failing to include meaningful metrics** — "recruiters and hiring managers care much more about the effort you put into quantifying your impact rather than the actual numbers themselves."
  4. **Not tailoring per role** — "focus your attention on three to five roles... invest more time in the quality of your application rather than the quantity." Paste similar JDs into a word-cloud generator to surface keywords.
  5. **Overlooking small things** — mismatched hyphens, typos. "first impressions matter and while perfect formatting won't guarantee you the interview, easily avoidable errors like this can be a red flag." Fix: "send your resume to 10 people you know... or at the very least use a tool like Grammarly."
- **LinkedIn vs. resume:** LinkedIn is "a more general overview of who you are professionally and it shouldn't change all that much, whereas your resume should be more hyper-targeted for each application."

### "Tell me about yourself" / structured thinking under pressure
He doesn't script a monologue — he teaches structure. He notes that "tell me about yourself" is one of the few questions where the interviewer *won't* wait while you structure your thoughts, so it's the one to have ready. For any *hard* / curveball question, run the 3-step process (stall → segment → answer). For any behavioral prompt, run CARL. Always articulate assumptions and logic out loud so the interviewer can follow — the process, not a "correct" number, is what earns the pass.

### AI mock-interview / prep loop
Use **Google's Interview Warm-up tool** (pick the role type; it runs an industry-specific mock and shows examples of strong answers) or role-play with Gemini/ChatGPT as the interviewer to "build confidence before the actual interview." Then feed the 6-Component formula: Persona = "act as a hiring manager," Task = "based on my resume and job description, ask me three questions I'm likely to face," Exemplar = your resume + the JD.

### Informational interview / coffee chat questions (3 categories + 1 non-negotiable closer)
- **Tasks & responsibilities:** "What aspect of your job do you find the most rewarding?" and "How is your performance measured? / What's your KPI?"
- **Career path:** "How did you begin your career?" and "What did you find the most surprising about the role?" (a nicer way to ask what they *don't* like).
- **Industry knowledge:** "What publications do you recommend I subscribe to...?" and "What are some current events or trends in [industry] I should be aware of?"
- **Always end with (non-negotiable):** *"Based on what we discussed today, is there anyone else you think I should talk to?"* — "By not asking this question, it's like leaving money on the table."
- **Coffee-chat mistakes to avoid:** not clarifying a time/topic commitment ("ask for and stick to 30 minutes"); putting up with long irrelevant tangents (redirect: "It's extremely impressive how you… I'm just trying to be mindful of time here… Would you mind talking a bit more about [X]?"); only bringing a smartphone for notes; not following up regularly (pick **three** people, note them in a simple Google Sheet, drop a note once a month/quarter/six months); a weak ending — reference one or two specific things they said.

### Cold email + networking with zero experience (367 cold emails in junior/senior year → 21 first-round interviews → 5 full-time offers)
**Cold email — 3 takeaways:** (1) "the objective of the cold email is to ask for a coffee chat or informational interview. Since research has shown that face-to-face requests are much more effective than emails" — so the CTA must include that objective; (2) "keep your self introduction to the minimum, and instead talk about things you two have in common. The more you do this, the more you show you've done your homework"; (3) "do not include a bunch of questions in your cold email... just get right to the point" (the coffee chat is where you talk about yourself).

**5 networking tips for students with zero experience:**
1. **Leverage your alumni database** — "go find your career center and ask them for your login information." Start freshman year, not junior. Pro tip: "identify relevant candidates on LinkedIn first, before searching for their names in the alumni directory."
2. **Leverage your professors** — "professors are more well-connected than you think." Go to office hours; ask them to connect you with previous students now in your target field.
3. **Attend as many events as possible, as early as possible** — "you need the practice." Talking to companies you're *not* interested in is "a risk-free environment because you have nothing to lose." His practiced line: "It was great chatting with you today, Jane. I'll let you go, since there are quite a few people lining up, would you mind terribly if I got your email or business card so I could follow up with questions?"
4. **Write an effective cold email** (see 3 takeaways above).
5. **Ask to be connected with someone else** — the single most important, lowest-effort move.
- His actual coffee-chat closer, verbatim: "Thanks again for your time. It's interesting how you're able to specialize in supply chain consulting after just two projects. Do you mind connecting me with someone who ended up specializing in the marketing and finance consulting teams? Just so I cover all my bases." Rationale: "you can always increase the surface area of which luck can hit you."

### Stand out at work / "Think Outside the Box" tactics
His recurring series is "Think Outside the Box... creative ways to stand out at work" — "using non-traditional methods to excel in the traditional professional workplace." Examples he actually used: a **mail merge** (personalized bulk emails) that earned "extra recognition at work" ("mail merge is the technical name for sending customized bulk emails. So if you want to sound smart at work, use that phrase"); building a **Google Sheets event-management structure** he could hand to cross-functional teams; and making free **Canva** assets (event posters, LinkedIn banner/headshot) when he "had no marketing support or budget" — "I received a lot of recognition for it at work because no one else had thought about something like this before." Underlying belief: presence in the workplace is legitimate — sending a project recap "is a very natural way to broadcast your achievements without coming off as inauthentic."

### Reaching out to recruiters (3-step, verbatim templates)
Core insight: "your message to the recruiter should end with a request to put you in touch with someone who's currently working the role that you're applying for." Three reasons it works: it shows you're proactive ("recruiters, like most people want to help those who help themselves"), it shows you can problem-solve, and it's polite/considerate — far better than "the generic, can I get 30 minutes to pick your brain message."
1. **Connect first, no ask** (relate / compliment / add value — don't mention the job): "Hi Annie. I see that we're both working for MNC tech companies and based out of Shanghai. I also notice you pursued your bachelor's degree at UNNC, where our company recently ran a college outreach program. Would love to connect if possible."
2. **Ask to be connected to someone in the role:** "Hi Annie, thanks for accepting my connection request. I saw a product marketing manager job posting from two weeks ago, and I'm currently doing a bit of research on the role to see whether I'd be a good fit. Would you happen to know anyone on the team I can have a casual chat with to better understand the position? As a fellow product marketer working in the tech space myself, I'd love to learn more about the day to day and team culture before I bother you with specific questions on the application process, thank you."
3. **Follow up to stay top of mind:** "Hi Annie, just wanted to close the loop and let you know my conversation with Jane went very well. Thank you again for putting us in touch... Chatting with Jane has only solidified my interest in joining Apple's product marketing team... and I'd love to send over my resume for your review if convenient. I sincerely believe the stakeholder management skills I've developed during my time as a product marketer at Google would transfer over well to this role."
- Even a rejected ask yields a good alternative — "I can answer any questions you might have" or "send over your resume first, I'll forward it along to the hiring manager" — "all of these alternatives are better than reaching out through a recruiter in a generic fashion." Always connect with someone doing the actual job — it's an "unfair advantage versus other candidates come interview time" and tells you if the day-to-day fits.

### LinkedIn profile (data-driven, from a large study + Austin Belcak)
Framing: most LinkedIn advice is "subjective — what works for me might not work for you," so lean on data.
- **Master keywords — 9 to 10 times per keyword.** Placement: once in the headline, "two to three times in the about section, three to five times in our experience section and one time in our skill section." This is *per keyword*, so three keywords → 27–30 total mentions. Find them by pasting five real target JDs into ChatGPT/Gemini: "analyze the five job descriptions... identify the top 10 keywords that appear by frequency... show a numerical value next to the keyword," then pick the three most relevant.
- **Two-part headline formula (Austin Belcak):** Part 1 = keyword-filled overview; Part 2 = value illustration with quantifiable results. Example: "Product Marketing | Digital Advertising | Helping small businesses 2x their return on ad spend." Three headline mistakes: don't use abbreviations (unless industry-standard), don't use just your current job title ("you want to stand out from the 99% of other users"), don't use "Seeking opportunities" or "Open to work" ("recruiters are not actively searching for those keywords").
- **About section (Austin's template, 4 parts):** (1) strong intro with keywords + measurable metrics; (2) three strongest achievements; (3) the sneaky open-to-opportunities line — "I get excited about opportunities where I get to do X" (signals availability without tipping off your employer); (4) contact info. Write in first person ("80% of respondents prefer profiles written in the first person"); most are 880–1,120 characters.
- **Skills — get endorsed:** individuals with "five or more skills receive 17 times more views." "linkedin's search algorithm will only factor in a skill if it has at least one endorsement," and endorsements "from professionals who already have those same skills" carry more weight.
- **"Steal with pride":** do an empty LinkedIn search → Posts → filter by target company → find active users who optimized their profiles → model five you resonate with. "you didn't have to reinvent the wheel."

### Project recap / status email (TL;DR-first)
Write it in a shared Google Doc, not straight into Gmail — "much easier for other people, like your manager, to review," easy for teammates to give input, and reusable next time. - **TL;DR** — "two to three sentences right at the beginning with quantifiable results." Purpose: "convey just enough information to help the reader decide whether or not they need to read the rest of the email." Seniors read only this; colleagues read on for the how.
- **Context** — "describes why the project was needed in the first place... the initial pain point you're trying to solve for."
- **Business Impact** — restate the objectives set at the start, then "share measurable results to provide an unbiased view on your performance," then add subjective commentary. Pro tip: "always include historical or industry numbers for benchmark purposes" ("1.2 million in sales would be terrible if we were at 1.5 million before").
- **Next Steps** — continue the project or not, and why (benefits/risks).
- **Takeaways & Learnings** — "the most important section but also the most difficult to get right." Highlights: only include "key activities that contributed towards" the original pain point. Lowlights: surface them by asking colleagues "if they were to work with you again, what they wish you would've done differently." Rule of thumb: "a maximum of three highlights and three lowlights."
- **Thank-yous** — executive sponsors, then key contributors ("ask your working group to add people here because you will definitely not remember everyone"), core team last, relevant docs linked at the bottom. Unwritten rule: name key contributors in the To field and CC their teams so their colleagues see the contribution.
- Why bother: benefits other teams, creates accountability, and is legit "presence in the workplace... a very natural way to broadcast your achievements without coming off as inauthentic."

### Self-assessment / performance review & onboarding (AI-assisted)
- **Self-assessment:** "our performance is measured based on a range of company characteristics, such as problem-solving, execution, and thought leadership." Copy the official attribute definitions from the company intranet + describe your projects, and have ChatGPT "match project content with company attributes, add some bureaucratic language, and most importantly, remind us to add quantifiable performance indicators in the right places."
- **30/60/90 onboarding plan (SMART):** "I've just become a key account manager in the sales team, and here are my KPIs... generate onboarding plans for 30, 60, and 90 days respectively, based on the SMART framework. Assign a metric to each goal so that I can objectively measure my performance. Output in tabular form." Then discuss the metric list with your manager — "they will definitely be impressed by your seriousness."
- **Intern → full-time (attitude, not output):** "As an intern, what matters isn't what you do... What matters is your work attitude and professional ethics." Same SMART 30/60/90 structure, quantified — "I contributed 5 user manuals and attended 90% of the non-mandatory training sessions" beats "I assisted with this project."
- **Present to your audience:** "the best speeches always connect what the speaker wants to express with what the audience wants to hear" — to a sales team, "establish a link between your product strategy and sales growth"; to executives, open with "thought-provoking questions" and keep it "interesting, informative, and relevant to them."
