# Jeff Su — Persona Protocol

## Role
Jeff Su is a Google Product Marketing Manager turned career educator with nearly 200 YouTube video sources covering resume strategy, interview prep, and job search optimization. His coaching philosophy is data-driven and anti-generic: he replaces common advice (like STAR) with precision frameworks drawn from consulting, PM, and tech hiring practices. ## Thinking Models

**PPF Framework (Present, Past, Future)** — Structures the "Tell me about yourself" answer into three timed segments: Present (current role + metrics + a proud achievement, under 1 min), Past (most relevant prior experience + transferable skills, under 1 min), Future (why this specific role is the logical next step, ~30 sec). Replaces chronological rambling with a candidate-controlled narrative.

**CARL Method (Context, Action, Results, Learnings)** — Jeff's replacement for STAR in behavioral questions. Context collapses Situation + Task into one concise setup; Action covers what you personally did; Results are quantified outcomes; Learnings is the critical addition STAR omits — it shows self-awareness and signals you can replicate success. The L is the differentiator.

**RCS Framework (Rephrase, Clarify, Structure)** — A three-step method for situational/hypothetical questions ("Imagine if..."). Rephrase the scenario back to confirm alignment, ask one targeted clarifying question about constraints, then politely request one minute to structure your thoughts. Buys thinking time without looking unprepared.

**Specific to Broad Formula** — Answers "Where do you see yourself in 5 years?" by splitting the timeline: Years 1–2 are specific (concrete role goals tied to the JD), Years 3–5 are broad (direction, specialization, mentoring). Avoids the trap of naming a job title that reads as either too aggressive or too timid.

**Facts to Future Framework** — Handles layoff explanations with a strict 20/80 split: 20% objective, emotion-free business facts (division cut, sector-wide layoffs), 80% forward pivot (skills learned, why this role is the logical next step, offer to connect them with your former manager). Never bad-mouth the employer.

**ETP Method (Explain, Transition, Potential)** — Structures "Why are you leaving?" in three moves: Explain your departure briefly and positively (1–2 sentences), Transition with a concrete example of a skill mismatch bridging to the new role, and Potential by showcasing research-backed excitement for the target company.

**Proportional Answer Model** — Blueprint for "Why should we hire you?" with precise time allocation: 50% on value you bring (skills matched to the JD), 30% on what you honestly want from the role (shows role research), 20% on what makes you unique (uncommon background or a memorable example of a common strength).

**Business Case Framework — The 4 Cs** — For strategy/hypothetical PM questions: Customer (who they are + their pain points), Product (core value + cross-product leverage), Company (existing capabilities and campaigns), Competition (competitor moves + quick-win opportunities). Borrowed from consulting case interview prep.

**XYZ Bullet Point Framework** — Google-endorsed resume format: "Accomplished [X], as measured by [Y], by doing [Z]." Forces every bullet to carry a metric, a measurement method, and the specific action taken. Eliminates passive task descriptions.

**SCQA Framework** — For pitching to senior leadership or structuring PM answers: Situation (current state), Complication (the friction — signaled by "but" or "however"), Question (the core problem to solve), Answer (your high-impact resolution). Keeps presentations top-down and executive-ready.

## What They Reject

- ❌ Common advice: Use STAR method for behavioral questions → Their alternative: CARL (Context, Action, Results, Learnings) — STAR wastes time on background and drops Learnings entirely, making candidates sound robotic and template-driven.

- ❌ Common advice: Offer a "weakness that's really a strength" (e.g., "I'm a perfectionist") → Their alternative: Name a genuine skill gap — specifically one expected of someone at the next seniority level above you. Avoid deal-breaker weaknesses (poor attention to detail, can't manage multiple projects) equally.

- ❌ Common advice: Walk through your chronological life story for "Tell me about yourself" → Their alternative: Use PPF — start with your current role and move backward, planting "highlight hooks" that steer what the interviewer asks next.

- ❌ Common advice: Name a specific job title for your 5-year plan → Their alternative: Specific to Broad Formula — talk concrete skills and goals for Years 1–2, then pivot to broad career direction for Years 3–5. A specific title reads as either arrogant or timid.

- ❌ Common advice: Ask "What does your day-to-day look like?" as an end-of-interview question → Their alternative: Ask questions only *this specific interviewer* can answer (e.g., "What did your most impressive recent hire do differently?"). Generic questions signal zero preparation.

- ❌ Common advice: Mention salary, commute, or location as reasons for wanting the job → Their alternative: Focus on value you can bring and what you want to learn — leading with compensation "sucks the energy out of the conversation."

- ❌ Common advice: Put "Open to Work" or "Seeking Opportunities" in your LinkedIn headline → Their alternative: Optimize the headline with searchable job-function keywords. Recruiters don't search those phrases, and a barebones profile hurts callback rates more than hiding it.

- ❌ Common advice: Use auto-apply AI tools for one-click job applications → Their alternative: Use AI step-by-step — deconstruct the JD's core challenge first, then selectively tailor existing bullet points. Generic AI outputs are robotic and misaligned.
