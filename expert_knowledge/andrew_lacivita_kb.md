### Core Thinking Models

- **The Great Eight (employer goals)** — the eight things every commercial organization cares about: (1) revenue generation, (2) market/brand awareness, (3) customer attraction, (4) customer happiness, (5) company growth (IPO/M&A/divestiture/funding), (6) employee happiness, (7) cost reduction, (8) process efficiency. *When to apply:* anchoring résumé bullets, interview stories, and salary arguments — everything you claim should ladder up to one of these.

- **The CAR Technique (Context → Approach → Result)** — his signature storytelling structure. Context first (why it mattered / the business problem), then Approach (your playbook: name the steps so you "come with a system"), then Result (the effect + benefits + any surprise upside). *When to apply:* any behavioral, project-specific, or situational interview question, and any résumé bullet.

- **Goals → Problems → Skills (the three dimensions)** — before you interview or write a résumé, work out the employer's top goals, the problems standing in the way of those goals, and the specific skills they'll want — then prove you've already solved those problems. *When to apply:* interview prep, résumé targeting, presentations mapping "how your skills fit."

- **Effect-then-Cause / "the echo, not the shout"** — every event has an "offspring"; lead with what happened *as a result* of what you did (the effect), then how you did it (the cause). *When to apply:* writing every résumé bullet and framing every accomplishment. Acid test: "What happened as a result of me doing what I did?"

- **The Career Achievements Journal (14 data points)** — capture 14 pieces of information per project (project, magnitude statistics, the problem, who was involved, the solution/steps, the outcome, the benefits, challenges encountered, how you overcame them, what you'd do differently, what the company learned, new skills *you* gained, how the company improved, your greatest victories). *When to apply:* building an inventory of vivid, substantiated stories for interviews, résumés, and promotions.

- **Sell to the Gap / Change the Value** — you sell fastest by selling to the employer's gap; and inside the interview you can *change what they think the role is worth* ("it's not 100 leads, I can get you 10,000") so they'll pay more. *When to apply:* positioning and salary negotiation.

- **UAECI (Unify → Act → Evaluate → Collect data → Iterate)** — his get-things-done loop: see everything as one unified goal, get in motion fast, evaluate, collect data, iterate on the *right* thing in the right sequence. *When to apply:* running a job search as a system instead of agonizing over one perfect artifact.

### Anti-Patterns (NEVER do these)

- NEVER volunteer an expected salary or salary range up front. "Your negotiation power does not come from what you earn." If pushed a third time, deflect with "Okay, I can see why that's important to you — what's your budget?" and give nothing.

- NEVER lead a résumé bullet or story with the activity ("I managed the customer service team"). That is "housekeeping" / "coma inducing" — lead with the *effect* (what happened as a result), then the cause.

- NEVER put "seeking new opportunities / open to work" on your public LinkedIn headline or summary — "nobody... searches for keywords that say seeking new opportunities," so it wastes your most prime real estate. Use the recruiter-facing "Open to Work" setting instead. "Put what you are, not what you're doing."

- NEVER accept concessions (remote days, extra vacation, a title) along the way — "you're collecting IOUs instead of collecting stack." Push everything to the end and batch the ask.

- NEVER counter-offer before you have the full *written* offer in hand, and NEVER counter on market/relative data ("so-and-so earns X") — argue on absolute future value.

- NEVER dump your résumé straight into the "applicant trashing system" as a first move, and NEVER eliminate a company on paper because of a requirement (travel %, location) before you've interviewed and reshaped what they want.

- NEVER answer "where do you see yourself in five years" with a specific title/spot — it reads as "unattainable or unavailable" and gets you selected out. Answer with *who* you want to be (trajectory), not *where*.

- NEVER chase or squeeze one opportunity to death with follow-ups while neglecting new send-outs — "press the start button," don't hit repeat.

## Detailed Frameworks & Playbooks

### 1. The Great Eight — What Employers Actually Want

Every commercial organization exists to serve customers and make money, and everything an employee could ever contribute breaks into eight buckets. Andy runs through them at nearly every live show because he "need[s] it to be drilled in your head." These are the through-line that connects the résumé, the interview stories, and the salary negotiation — "there is a Continuum... it needs to be continuous and consistent across all of these."

1. **Revenue generation** — top line, "make the money." You do NOT need to be on the sales team: sales support, proposal development, building the demo the account executive takes to the meeting, the systems engineer on the sales call, upselling on the customer service team, customer-success outreach — all count.
2. **Market / brand awareness** — "a lift in recognition." SEO, social content, white papers, podcasts, conferences, billboards, rebranding, migrating from a legacy brand.
3. **Customer attraction** — inside sales, prospecting, lead magnets ("a little honey out there"), sample packets, outreach programs.
4. **Customer happiness** — customer service, customer success, product refinement, self-service tools. "There's not too many people that exist in an organization that don't in some way shape or form touch this one."
5. **Company growth** — IPO, mergers, acquisitions, divestitures, private equity / venture capital, plus risk management, safety, legal, audit, compliance. (An accountant who caught an audit discrepancy averted a stock hit — that's company growth.)
6. **Employee happiness** — career development models, evaluation/promotion processes, rewards programs, employee stock purchase plans, recruiting the right people, quarterly team events.
7. **Cost reduction** — expense management, cost elimination, swapping people for a system, AI tools, reallocating/cross-training staff to avoid recruitment fees.
8. **Process efficiency** — streamlining any process; the accountant who cut a 10-day, 10-person closing cycle to 5 days ("100 people-days" → "50 people-days"), automating a manual process, condensing/eliminating steps, reducing errors.

To find *your* contribution to any of the eight, brainstorm with three questions:
> "Did I do the main thing? ... Did I do a step, an intermediary step, a pre-step? ... Or was there an ingredient?"

Example — the "main thing" is the salesperson closing the deal; a "prerequisite step" is building the demo the sales team carried into the meeting; an "ingredient" is the white paper the content manager later used to attract customers. On the résumé, the Great Eight *effect* leads and "how you did that... is less powerful but offers some context."

> "The fact that you manage the customer service team just tells me you manage the customer service team. It doesn't really tell me what you accomplished as a result of managing the customer service team."
> "It's in the echo, not to shout... every event that you are in has an offspring of some sort."
> "These grade eights... all of these are major accomplishments. That's where you win the discussion, that's where you get the interview, that's how you win the interview."

### 2. The CAR Technique — Context, Approach, Result

His single best interview tip ("pound for pound the best interview tip you will ever get"). It covers all three interview question formats — behavioral ("tell me about a time when..."), project-specific ("walk me through how you did that"), and situational ("how would you do this?").

- **Context (king and queen and checkmate):** paint why you were even doing this — the business problem — in ~15–20 seconds *before* any steps. The mistake most people make is starting "I did step one, then step two, then step three," leaving the interviewer with "no idea where you are in your story."
  > Example: "We had this customer experience system... people were on hold too long, so we were losing a large 10% of our customers every month — it was costing us a million a month." → "Whoa, sounds like a big problem, right? ... Now they know you're a badass."

- **Approach (your playbook):** name the steps up front so the listener has a map, then weave back through with "detailed actions."
  > "CAR stands for context, approach — the first part of the approach is your playbook, your framework, your system... everything you did has a number of steps."

  Reverse-engineer whatever you do into a methodology: "get with the stakeholders, discover the issues, build the requirements, design it, architect it, build it, test it, put it into place." Naming steps makes you sound "systematic, more logical, more organized" and signals you "come with a playbook." "They pay more for systems as opposed to point solutions." (Language matters: "you didn't *change* the way you got data into the contracts — you *re-engineered* it.")

- **Result:** the outcome + benefits + "any additional surprises that work to your favor." "Six months later we turned on the new system... we were only losing 2% of our customers, which is natural attrition — so I ended up getting back $800,000, $900,000."

Bonus tactic — **prepare behavioral questions INSIDE your project-specific stories.** A bad interviewer asks "tell me about a challenge you overcame" and leaves you "wrecking your brain fishing around." The best interviewer asks you to walk through a project and then interrupts at step two or three: "what was the biggest challenge you faced when you were corralling the stakeholders?" So if you prep your behavioral answers *within* the projects that matter to the employer, "you will never be surprised, you will never be thrown off guard."

> "Context is king and queen and checkmate, because once they understand why you did what you did and you tell them how you did what you did with all the parameters racked together, you're going to get hired."

Watch his companion videos in your own retrieval: "the job interview formula guaranteed to get you hired" and "two questions to ask in a job interview that will get you hired."

### 3. You Sell Transformation, Not Skills (Goals → Problems → Skills)

The drill analogy: "You can sell the drill and its features. You can sell the hole in the wall that it's going to create... or you can sell the feeling of looking at that picture every day." Candidates make the fatal mistake of leading with skills/features. Step back and be the *customer*: "Anything that you buy in your life, why do you buy it? ... It's transformation. It's what it's going to do for you."

> "They do not buy your skills. They do not buy features... you buy transformation, right?"
> "When I don't have any frame of reference in the application of your skills or experience, they are meaningless to me."

**The three dimensions you must work out in advance:**
1. **Goals** — "everything starts with the goals." They're easy and straightforward; don't overcomplicate. The marketing team always wants more leads, faster, cheaper, converting higher. You can even ask an AI tool: "I'm on the marketing team — what are the marketing team's top three goals?"
2. **Problems** — harder, because every team's problems differ. Build "a pretty holistic inventory" of the problems that could stand in the way. Best of all is surfacing **"latent pain"** they don't even know they have: "then you're an absolute genius and you'll get hired... their lack of awareness gives you an opportunity."
3. **Skills** — "not what skills do I have, what skills do they *want*." "Anything any of you do I can spin into anything that any employer wants for that particular role if you know how to shine a light on it." Don't rely on the job description to hand you the skills list — a customer service rep needs empathy, communication, influence, organization, product knowledge, follow-through, and knowledge-base building; the JD rarely names all of them.

**Sequence for a "map my skills to the job" presentation:** (1) the employer's **goals**, (2) their **problems**, (3) your **power story / case study** in CAR form, (4) the **specific outcomes**, (5) *only then* the **skills** you used — by which point "I will have already assumed you have all the skills I need. At this point, all you're doing is reiterating." Do NOT present skills first: "big mistake." Leave behind a one-slide **quad** per case study: upper-left = scenario (goals + problems), then solution/process/methodology, then resources/skills used, then results and benefits — "and here's a pretty little picture."

A "power story" (for those who don't "speak Coach Andy") = the accomplishment in your background "that is as close as possible to what you need to do in the future with that employer." While you tell it, "I'm deducing your skills... she must know this, she must be organized, she must be influential" — "by the time you get to the fifth point, you've already won it."

### 4. Effect-then-Cause Résumé Writing (and Quantification)

The résumé is "a marketing document, not a work history document" — "it's about where you want to go... it uses where you've been to justify and tease and imply." Build it *after* you determine your destination, not before. "The single greatest thing you can do for your résumé to give it the most power" is aligning your accomplishments to the Great Eight.

**The career profile is the most important section** — the "billboard," the "book cover," the top half of the first page. A recruiter can decide "with 90% certainty after just looking at somebody's career summary" whether to invite you. It "controls the narrative" and "sets my biases." Without it, the recruiter has to build your story for you, "taking longer to construct a more inaccurate story about you."

Why placement matters: before a recruiter even opens your résumé, the hiring official has told them exactly what to look for — "business-to-business... manufacturing... software as a service... 10 years... small to medium-sized company." "When a recruiter looks at your résumé, they're not looking at what you are writing, they're looking at what the hiring official told them to look for." So put the right descriptors in the right spots — chiefly the career profile.

Career profile formula (answers ~10 questions in ~1.5 inches): 2–3 word descriptor + relative years of experience + industries/markets you know best + your major aggregate accomplishment ("I've helped more than 100,000 people find new jobs") + **core competencies** (which are *business functions, not character traits* — "you are not a leader on paper"; write "cost-based accounting, CRM, complex sales, budgeting/forecasting") + technical skills/languages + top educational credential (MBA from Harvard goes at the *top* for professionals, not a bottom education section).

**Every bullet in between profile and education is effect then cause:**
- "Raised customer satisfaction 39.7% by streamlining X." The activity ("managed the customer service team") goes at the *end* or into "housekeeping."
- Start with a real action verb — "save, optimize, reduced" — "not ran the team."
- Reviewers read down the *left half* of the page, so "the money part should be" on the left.
- **Acid test:** "What happened as a result of me doing what I did? If it doesn't tell them this, it ain't done... there's no power, there's no fangs in those bullets."
- Always give the **Delta**: "If you say you managed a $30 million portfolio... was it $40 million when you started, or was it zero? I need to understand the delta."

**Quantify** by magnitude, rationale, and estimate — "don't agonize if you don't have the exact data... I'm not saying lie... the quantification serves as a frame of reference and order of magnitude and context."
- The CIO who cut a 2-week billing cycle to 20 minutes → estimate: a $150M company makes ~$12M/month; if $6M is delayed, "you accelerated collections... $6 million cash flow problem."
- The disability case manager who thought there was "no percent value to add" → quantify *volume*: "300 people who are in better shape because of you."

AI tip: don't ask ChatGPT to "write me a bullet." Ask it "what are the top three goals of any marketing professional?" and "what are the biggest challenges companies have in achieving those goals?" — use it to work out goals and problems, then you supply the substance. "If you don't actually think about what it is that employers need... you're not going to get the job."

### 5. The Career Achievements Journal (14 pieces of information)

Keep a running diary/journal (spreadsheet, Word doc, Evernote — "whatever the fad app of the day is") capturing 14 data points *per project* so your stories have "vivid color," your résumé bullets have detail, and you can make your case for a job *or* a promotion. A "project" is "a unique contained set of activities" — it can be a year long or three days long; making 10,000 widgets is a project; so is streamlining the process the other reps use.

1. **Project(s)** you worked on
2. **Statistics** for magnitude ("$3 million... 50 people... four city blocks") — anything that lets the listener *see* the size
3. The **problem** you were solving / improving / eradicating — "any minute of any day, you're solving a problem"
4. **Who was at the party** — partnerships, vendors, task forces, the steering committee you chaired (this alone makes the interviewer "draw conclusions about your abilities... you must have good senior executive presence")
5. The **solution / steps** — your sequence (also your CAR "approach" and your template for behavioral, project-specific, and situational answers)
6. **What happened** — the outcome (the STAR "R" / his "outcome"); and "just because you stopped somewhere in the chain doesn't mean you didn't have an outcome"
7. The **benefits** and *who* benefited, and the delta — "this is the first piece of information in your résumé bullet"
8. **Challenges encountered** along the way — distinct from the initial problem ("the software was late" is a challenge; slow customer response was the problem)
9. **How you overcame** them — "I had a risk plan with mitigating factors and a contingency patch" → "think about how awesome you sound... I know how to manage risk, I know how to anticipate stuff"
10. What you'd **do differently**
11. What the **company learned** — "what does your company know now that it didn't before you started?"
12. What **new skills YOU** can now do — "if your number 12 is not a good list, you're in a job where you're not really growing"
13. How the **company improved** — what it can do now that it couldn't
14. Your **greatest victories** — real even if the project was never implemented ("it was a real victory because I lived it for 14 months")

You don't need "14 for 14" on every project — "I'm giving you the kitchen sink."

> "It makes no difference how successful you are, you will never feel successful unless you reflect on what you've done."
> "The most important thing about storytelling... is that you can substantiate your claims — not whether it was 1.5 million or 1.2 million."
> "Reflection is about having the right perspective in evaluating dispassionately what happened... if you are feeling worse, you're doing it wrong."

### 6. Mileage Not Age / Repetitions Beat Calendar Time

Don't answer "yes, that's part of what I do" — it "poo-poos it" and downplays you, especially if you're junior. Convert experience into *reps*.
> Example (24-year-old, one year of experience): "For each project there are five steps... the fourth step, because you asked me directly, is the review session about risk with the doctors — usually a four-hour session, I'm speaking to somewhere between eight and a dozen doctors, and over the course of the year I've done that eight times." — versus the flat "yes, that's part of what I do."

> "Mileage not age... I want the person who reps it, not the person who's got 15 years and does one project a year."
> "500 people a year — you are not showing up on a Zoom with me and going to surprise me... I just have too much data at my fingertips up in this skull-shaped kingdom."

Two more things "senior and smart people do": be **consultative** (ask about the employer's problems, diagnose "just like a doctor would") and be **industry-trend knowledgeable** — always present information before you make an ask ("I've noticed the industry is moving toward X — has your company started to do that?").

### 7. Job Search as a System (UAECI + Boss Hunting)

**Target companies, not roles.** "Don't you worry your pretty little self about whether they've got an opening or not." Add companies to the list when you stumble on an open role or a connection who works somewhere good.

**Pecking order to reach in:**
1. **The boss** (boss hunting) — "works if and only if you have requisite skills that boss will need at this moment in time... good bosses are always on the lookout for great people even if they don't have open positions."
2. **A teammate** — ask for their *insight* ("I wanted to check in to see how you enjoy working there"), build a dialogue, then: "would you benefit if I was one of your referrals?" — a yes/no question, "not a will-you-submit-me."
3. **Recruiters / HR** — last, because a recruiter with open reqs "for other hiring officials" is lower priority than a boss who could create a slot for you.
4. **The ATS** — an absolute last resort. "I would be willing to take months before I ever put my résumé into an applicant trashing system." (Stat he cites: ~8% of people found their job via LinkedIn unsolicited vs. <1% aggregate via the ATS.)

**Cadence:** send three messages to three people at three companies *every day.* "Press the start button" every day instead of the repeat button — be "a marketing machine, not being played on replay." Spend ~90% of your time on send-outs (targeted networking/boss-hunting messages), not agonizing over follow-ups with people who aren't responding.

**The UAECI loop:** **Unify** everything toward the one goal (every interview, every company feeds one common goal) → **Act** fast (ship v1.0; "if the résumé takes longer than a day it's taking you too long") → **Evaluate** (even on limited data — "pay attention to what happened") → **Collect data** (who, what medium, what day, what level) → **Iterate** on the *right* thing in the *right* sequence. "A lot of you are iterating in the wrong spot" (perfecting the résumé, chasing non-responders) instead of doing more send-outs.

When a boss redirects you to the ATS, that's a *yes* — comply *and* immediately ask for the recruiter's name so you can follow up ("so-and-so told me to put my résumé in"), which implies you know that person. Automate a 30-day boilerplate follow-up. "Andy LaCivita school of living life: any action... the immediate reaction is 'I'm sure that was for my own good' — life happens for me, not to me. What can I do with this right now?"

The **free 5-Day Job Search Challenge** structure he teaches: Mon = the blueprint, Tue = identify target companies, Wed = identify people to reach out to, Thu = exactly what to say, Fri = all the problems you'll have and how to solve them.

> "Don't wait for your A game when your B game will change lives."
> "You can't edit a blank page."
> "Consistency trumps talent... 80% every day of quality product is going to far supersede when you aggregate it all together."

### 8. Evaluating the Employer — Detective Work
Great brands "heal quickly"; weak organizations "seek the quick fix" and lurch "from one crisis mode to another" (the Chicago pothole analogy — one bandaid after another until you're "driving down a rocky road"). Ask multi-part questions — "why did that happen? how did that occur? why is that important? what are you doing next?" — to see beneath "the iceberg." "It's not just what they're telling you... it's the iceberg effect."

Read the **digital footprint**: Does the careers page invest in management/employee/customer profiles and testimonials ("I don't care how private you are, you got to have this kind of stuff")? Does the job description spend ~50% selling *why it's a great place to work*, not just piling on demands (and "no job description should be longer than one page")? Is there a visible career path? "Are they hiring a lot of salespeople? Two kinds of companies do that — the kind that are growing or the kind that are losing salespeople all the time." This is "listening to what you're seeing" — investigate, don't just apply. Glassdoor is weak signal ("malcontents go out there and write a lot of that stuff") but consistent patterns are worth noting.

Beware your *own* **quick-fix syndrome** — desperate job seekers "become more susceptible" to jumping badly. "So too do job seekers who make poor choices about the organizations they join."

### 9. The Six Hidden Mistakes (one per job-search stage)
A named checklist he teaches — "mistakes we don't know we're doing":
1. **Congruence (Step Zero)** — think/say/do must be in sync across what you want, the résumé, and the interview stories. "Employers can smell the inconsistency."
2. **Requirements not listed in nauseating detail** — list must-haves / nice-to-haves and the *questions* you'll ask to test them, so you're not blindsided by **recency bias** (over-weighting what bugs you now, forgetting what you take for granted).
3. **Cause instead of effect on the résumé** — "the effect is the money part, literally and figuratively... don't bury the lead."
4. **Pressing repeat instead of start** — "be a marketing machine, not being played on replay." Keep hitting *start* (new send-outs) every day; don't squeeze one lead to death.
5. **Leaving doubt in the interview** — the dryer/wire story; always ask "do you have any reservations about hiring me?"
6. **Negotiating unknowingly along the way** — accepting concessions early "knocks a limb off" your leverage. Push it all to the end.

### 10. Mindset — Roots, Subtraction, and Being a Threat to Average
- **Work on the roots, not the tree** — success is the tree everyone admires; the roots (the invisible skill-building) are what make it grow. "There is somebody out there who's way less talented than you... living the life you want, and the only difference is they're working on the roots." "You are all one skill away from doubling your salary."
- **Addition by subtraction** — "the reason most people do not achieve their goals is because they're unwilling to give up the things they need to give up to create the space." Every day he cuts something. Filter what enters your life through the "ITTIES" (identity, community, ability, necessity) — "everything that comes at me is an automatic no to begin with... I would rather go from a no to a yes than a yes to a no."
- **Don't interrupt yourself** — "success has its own timetable; if you stick with it you'll be around to see it when it shows up" (the recruiter Jen who quit weeks before eight placements would have paid her).
- **It's in the ripples / focus on the offspring** — you predict the future by evaluating the *offspring* of an event, not forecasting ("focus on the shout when you're doing it; when you're reflecting, focus on the echo").
- **Give more than you think is reasonable**, and **do what you were meant to do** — "just because you can doesn't mean you should... don't let what you can do stand in the way of what you were meant to do."
> "How can I be a threat to everything average?"
> "The victory is found in the things you don't do." (Ashley, who rejected an offer even after they raised it — "a topshelf company" — because it wasn't right.)

## Signature Question Playbook

### "Tell me about yourself" / positioning
Lead with the **transformation** and trajectory, not a chronology. Establish who you are (a verb-noun combo — "I coach people"), the scale of what you've done, and the value you create — congruent with where you want to go. Beware incongruence in "tell me about yourself" when "you start telling them things that... are not really what they need or how you advertised yourself." Keep **think, say, do** aligned:
> "You will never be more on track than when those three things are all the same."

### "Where do you see yourself in five years?"
He calls this question "epically stupid," but here's the fix: never name a **where** (a title/spot). Human nature makes the interviewer judge it as **"unattainable"** ("there's no way she goes from systems engineer to CIO in five years") or **"unavailable"** ("we only have one CIO, he's not going anywhere") — and "the more ambitious you are, the more hosed you are." Instead, answer with **who** you want to be — the trajectory.
> Script skeleton: "I want to work in a capacity where I can [add value / help people] by [the value you contribute] so that [the beneficiary — the company, the market, the world] leads a rewarding career and fulfilled life."

The magic is the trajectory, and it doubles as your elevator pitch / LinkedIn headline / networking + informational-interview intro (where people also default to naming a spot).
> "Don't let the how drive the what. Most people throw in the towel before they start any big project because they can't see all the steps."
> "The joy is in the doing."

### Behavioral questions ("tell me about a time...")
Two layers. (a) The behavioral **framework** — explain your **philosophy**, then your **process**, then the **outcome**: "in my opinion the best way to answer a behavioral interviewing question." (b) Deliver it in **CAR**. The dreaded STAR "R" (result) = his "outcome."

Model **"team player"** answer (a "multi-use response" that also covers disagreement/common-goal questions): "The way I'm a team player is because I'm always focusing on the common and greater goal the team has... when teammates start disagreeing, the best thing a team player can do is step back, listen... and look at developing a solution that accommodates the input of all parties in alignment with the common goal." The best supporting story is a *disagreement* where "we listened to each other, kept focusing on the common goal, and combined our two opinions into an even better solution." That answer quietly conveys "I'm a team player, I'm level-headed, I'm a great listener, and I always look at the big picture." (Note: an "I influenced someone to do what I wanted" story shows *influencing* skills, not *team-player* skills — pick the right story for the trait being tested.)

### Nerves & confidence
His three/four-part recipe: (1) **be completely focused on what you're doing**, not the outcome — "you can't lose something you technically don't have. You have nothing to lose, you don't have the job yet." (Choking = "thinking about something other than what you're doing," like the golfer imagining the trophy instead of the putt.) (2) **Serve them** — "when you go on a job interview, you're actually serving them... presenting yourself as an opportunity to go there and help them." (3) **Be "damn ready"** — know your stories, prepare for the most common questions. (4) Think **"this company would be lucky to have me"** — "not in an obnoxious way."

For online interviews there are two proficiencies — the *mechanics* (rehearse to camera in short bursts over a few days until it's "totally natural") and *content knowledge*. Keep your notes/job description on-screen to glance at.
> "My mind is open to any possibility but my heart is attached to no outcome. I will not give in."
> "I don't care what happens" — refuse to let an outcome you can't control control you.

### The high-stakes close — "Do you have any reservations about hiring me?"
Never leave doubt. The dryer analogy: a $41 corroded wire kills a fully working machine — "you left them with doubt... you didn't ask them do you have any reservations about hiring me." Asking surfaces one of three things: (1) they *miscommunicated* — you said something they misinterpreted, so clean it up; (2) they never asked and you never got to answer a concern, so clarify it now; or (3) it's a real *reservation* you can dampen ("I might not have that skill, but I have this one over here").
> "Do not walk out unless all the wires are touching, you got heat in the dryer."

### Salary — the full playbook (his "Baker's Dozen")
Core belief: what you *earn* and what you *deserve* are irrelevant; you get paid on the **future value** you'll create and how well you prove it. "There will always be money for the right person, and if you're going to tell me that's not true, I'm going to tell you you just didn't dazzle them enough to make them want to dig deep into their pockets."

- **Step Zero — take stock of your current total *value package*.** "You don't make $120,000" if it's a $100k base plus a bonus you got 5% of. Know your salary, bonus timing, 401(k) match, PTO, stipends. "No two job offers will ever be equal."
- **Write your goals for the position** (via the Great Eight + the JD) and **keep a diary** of every pain point, aspiration, and "what's been missing on the team and in the candidates you've interviewed." You'll pull this list out at the counter-offer to make a "cohesive, lucid argument."
- **The salary-avoidance script (three-ask defense):** "Okay, that sounds like a reasonable place to start, let's go." If pushed again: "No, I really mean that." Third ask: "Okay, I can see why that's important to you — what's your budget?" If they *still* refuse a range, "that's a huge red flag." If a number is truly forced, give your **actual current salary**, never an "expected salary" — "one is irrelevant and the other can only do harm."
- **When they tell you the posted salary in the interview, slide right past it** — "water off your back. I don't even care what they say it is." Don't prematurely eject: "you may think it's worth 75, but you haven't seen me yet."
- **Be a transparent communicator throughout** — tell them every company/position you're interviewing for and that things are "pretty good" where you are ("I'm not running out of a burning building"). It earns a reputation as a communicator, buys grace time later, and lets you close with "you hit this number, I'll drop them all."
- **Never accept concessions along the way** — remote Fridays, extra PTO, a title bump. "You're collecting IOUs instead of collecting stack." When the employer negotiates *with themselves* ("I'm sure we can bump that vacation to three weeks"), deflect: "I'm sure we'll talk about that at the end." **Push, push, push all the way to the end** — never say "thank you," "that sounds great," or anything that reads as acceptance.
- **You're not at the negotiating table until you have a full *written* offer.** At the offer, ask **questions only, for information** first (when/how is the bonus paid, what did the team get last year, does the match vest, is it Phantom or incentive stock?) — "they will start negotiating with themselves in their own head... 'God, this doesn't sound as good as I thought it was.'"
- **Counter on absolute future value, not market/relative data.** "You've been losing $300,000 a month — if I get all that back in a day, am I not worth a million?" Highlight unique value: "I'm taking that $150,000 resource off the table — you don't have to pay me an extra 150, but you can pay me more." Relative arguments ("so-and-so earns X") are weak because the market shifts and "I don't know how hard that job is."
- **Everything is negotiable** — signing bonuses, "pay for change" (relocation/pain-of-change pay), two-year relocation windows (a bootcamper got exactly that), extra PTO. Give control of *how* they fund it: "however you want to get me the extra $50 grand is up to you."
- **The close that gets the last dollar:** "Here's the value I'll add... you hit this number and I'll drop all the others — I give you my word, here's my start date." The extra $10k they add "buys off risk" — the risk of losing you and starting over — "you're not worth $10 grand more, but the whole situation is worth $10 grand more to me."

> "Your negotiation power does not come from what you earn... it comes from how you prove yourself."
> "The why helps them solve your solution" — always give a "because" (e.g., "because I don't have to move") so they can find creative ways to satisfy the ask.
> "Assumptions are not your friends. Haggle it out at the end, go when your stock is the highest, make your argument and stand your ground."
> "The victory is found in the things you don't do" — walking away is a win.

### "Why are you leaving / how do I know it's time to go?"
From *Interview Intervention*, page 12: "Most people never act on new adventures. It's my opinion that they would rather live with unhappiness than uncertainty... they hold themselves hostage with decisions they made during their twenties serving as the shackles." Diagnostic pecking order — separate *what* you do from *whom* you do it with:
- If **what you do** doesn't bring you joy → change job function or career.
- If you like the work but not "these idiots you're working with" → change companies.
- Before leaving, try to change what's changeable ("when a husband and wife have an argument, you don't just go get a divorce"); if it's unchangeable, go. Give a place *one* shot to fix a raised issue.

Choose the next role by reverse-engineering your **requirements first** ("you don't just start looking for a job"), listed in "nauseating detail," then use that criteria to target *and* evaluate companies. Some criteria (industry, market, company type) you can judge "from the comfort of your own desk"; culture, values, and integrity "you won't know until you actually get in and evaluate them." Beware **recency bias** — over-weighting the one thing that bugs you now and forgetting the things you take for granted (which then vanish at the new job because they weren't on your list).

### Resigning
Give exactly **two weeks** — "you don't need to give more than two weeks... half the time they want to walk you out before then anyway." Prepare a written resignation letter but don't email it before you speak (to your boss if the relationship is good, otherwise HR). Say only: "I'm resigning, my last day is [date], I have accepted another job." Never "I'm thinking about" or "waiting for an offer" — "signed, sealed and delivered." If asked why (and you choose to share), give a reason they *cannot* address ("I've always wanted to work in this area of technology and since we don't do that here...") so they can't counter-offer you into staying — "employers do not like to be fired; they want to get rid of you on their time, not your time." A chapter on this is in *Interview Intervention*.

### Disclosing salary history (nuance)
Default: **do not disclose** in today's environment ("laws have changed... why take the chance"). Exceptions where volunteering *helps you* and reveals your mindset: unvested RSUs, a built sales pipeline/commissions, or a bonus about to pay ("I have a $50,000 payday on January 31st — I'm not walking away from that; would you like to match it and I'll come a month early?"). And when a company says "we can't raise your salary," the word **because** must follow — force a rationale exchange ("because that would be unfair to everyone who's already proven those skills" / "because I don't have to move — cover it with a signing bonus / call it pay-for-change pay").
