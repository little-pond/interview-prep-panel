### **Core Frameworks & Formulas**

*   **The CAR (Challenge, Action, Result) / PAR (Problem, Action, Result) 
Storytelling Framework**: Madeline advises throwing out the traditional STAR 
method, calling it a flashy acronym over-focused on branding [1-3]. STAR has 
four steps (Situation, Task, Action, Result), which sets candidates up to 
over-explain context and ramble [1, 3-5]. Instead, she champions this 
streamlined three-step method to keep stories concise, focusing on the most 
valuable elements—your actions and the results [1, 4, 6, 7].
*   **The "Tell Me About Yourself" (or "Walk Me Through Your Resume") 3-Part 
Formula**: A formula designed to deliver a highly relevant, professional pitch 
in under two minutes to help context-switching interviewers get oriented [8-13]:
    *   *Part 1: Who You Are:* A concise professional identity sentence aligned 
with the target role, using a "through line" to connect past experiences if you 
are pivoting fields [8, 14-17].
    *   *Part 2: Highlight Reel:* A reverse-chronological highlight of 2 to 4 
quantifiable accomplishments from your past few roles [8, 16, 18-20].
    *   *Part 3: Why This Role:* A 1-to-2 sentence conclusion explaining why 
this specific company and role represent the logical next step in your career 
trajectory [8, 21-24].
*   **The "Why Should We Hire You?" 3-Step Formula**: A framework that 
eliminates the pressure of bragging or delivering a generic sales pitch [25-30]:
    *   *Step 1: Review the Role:* Reiterate what you know about the role and 
the specific business challenges it faces [27, 31-34].
    *   *Step 2: Relevant Experience:* Match your specific skills and past 
accomplishments directly to those challenges [27, 31, 35-37].
    *   *Step 3: Ask Your Interviewer:* Turn the question into a two-way 
conversation by asking, *"Is this how you see me in this role?"* or *"Did I miss
any key challenges?"* [35, 38-42]. This lets you address any misalignments on 
the spot [38-40, 42, 43].
*   **The CTO Test (Character, Thinking, Outcome)**: An advanced framework 
designed for executive-level candidates (Director, VP, C-level) to elevate 
behavioral storytelling [44]:
    *   *Character (C):* Detailing how your actions in stories implicitly prove 
positive traits (such as empathy, strategic thinking, or being hardworking) 
rather than listing empty adjectives [45, 46].
    *   *Thinking (T):* Bringing the listener along your strategic thought 
process and detailing how you made calculated decisions [46].
    *   *Outcome (O):* Sharing high-level, strategic results of your actions, 
while taking a moment to throw compliments back to your team [47].
*   **The Leadership Style Response Framework**: A four-step blueprint to answer
leadership questions: 1) emphasize flexibility (there is no one-size-fits-all 
style), 2) identify diverse leadership moments from your past (even leading 
external vendors), 3) provide concrete CAR stories proving those management 
styles, and 4) explicitly tie it back to the company's specific culture [48, 
49].
*   **Question Layering**: A formula to turn basic end-of-interview questions 
into impressive inquiries [50]. By adding one or more of three 
layers—**Research** (something you learned beforehand), **Experience** 
(referencing your past), or **Interview Context** (referencing a previous 
conversation in the loop)—you speak like an active future co-worker [51, 52].

### **Mental Models & Mindset Shifts**

*   **Job Seeking vs. Job Shopping**: The core philosophy of shifting from a 
passive "job seeker" (mass-applying, waiting for recruiters to choose you, and 
treating the interview like a test) to a "job shopper" who is clear on what they
want, optimizes their brand for inbound outreach, and treats interviews as a 
collaborative vetting process to assess mutual fit [53-59].
*   **Autobiography Syndrome**: The trap where candidates view their resume and 
interview responses as an exhaustive, chronological history of their lives 
[60-62]. Because hiring managers do not have time to piece your history 
together, you must treat your narrative as a **sales page** focused strictly on 
how you can solve their future business problems [13, 60, 61, 63].
*   **Every Job Interview is a Concert**: A model to overcome interview fatigue 
[64, 65]. Reframe the interview: first, remember the interviewer "bought 
tickets" to your show by dedicating their valuable time and desperately wants it
to be a great show [64, 65]; second, even if you have played your "setlist" of 
career hits a thousand times, your audience is brand new and wants to hear your 
greatest hits played with fresh enthusiasm [64, 66].
*   **Assume Positive Intent / Expectancy Effects**: A model to avoid 
self-sabotage under pressure [67, 68]. Instead of perceiving repeated questions 
or blunt comments as a sign the interviewer dislikes you, reframe them as 
invitations to succeed [67-72]. Assume they like you, want you to succeed, and 
are actively prompting you to improve your answer so they can easily advocate 
for you to the rest of the hiring panel [69-72].
*   **The "I over We" Rule**: While teams are important, overusing "we" in an 
interview dilutes your impact and leaves interviewers confused about what you 
actually did [73, 74]. She teaches a strict "I over we" rule to ensure you 
explicitly claim your individual contributions and strategic accomplishments 
[73, 74].
*   **"I Just" Syndrome**: A self-sabotaging mental habit where candidates 
diminish their own value by stating *"I just helped on that project"* or *"I 
just did that for a fundraiser"* [75]. Madeline counsels to kick this syndrome 
to the curb, claim your accomplishments, and Rebrand yourself as a problem 
solver [75, 76].

### **Step-by-Step Methods**

*   **Building a Story Toolbox**: A systematic preparation method to ensure you 
have specific, tangible examples ready for any behavioral question [77-79]:
    1.  *Anthropologize old work:* Rummage through old files, texts, emails, and
past resumes to dig up details of past projects [77, 80].
    2.  *Gather core stories:* Outline at least two problem-solving "hero" 
accomplishments, one interpersonal story (teamwork, communication, or conflict),
and one failure/mistake story [77, 81].
    3.  *Draft in CAR/PAR:* Write out bullet points focusing heavily on your 
actions and results [81-83].
    4.  *Use "Titling":* Assign a simple, three-word title to each story in your
notes (e.g., *"Software System Rollout"*) [84, 85].
    5.  *Scan and Select:* During the interview, glance down at your story 
titles, select the most relevant story, and adapt its introduction to perfectly 
fit the question asked [84-86].
*   **The PAD Mindset System (to handle nerves)**: A three-step framework to 
"pad" your mind against physiological and psychological pressure [87]:
    *   *P – Prepare:* Deeply study the job description to align all of your 
answers to their top three hard skills and top three soft skills, and research 
the business [88].
    *   *A – Adjust:* Write down your negative, nervous thoughts and reframe 
them into positive peer-to-peer mantras to repeat to yourself during prep and 
right before the interview [89].
    *   *D – Desensitize:* Force yourself to experience nervousness in 
low-stakes situations to desensitize your brain (e.g., practicing in a mirror, 
filming mock video sessions of yourself, practicing with others in the room, and
never turning down a real interview opportunity) [89-92].
*   **The T-Chart Method**: Reread the job description like a detective [60, 
93]. Make a two-column T-chart: on the left, list each bullet responsibility of 
the role; on the right, map your exact qualifications and accomplishments [93, 
94]. When you walk into the interview, restrict your comments *strictly* to the 
details on the right side of the T-chart—if a detail didn’t make it on there, 
the company does not care about it [95, 96].
*   **Menu Answers (To Stop Rambling)**: To stop rambling on broad, vague 
questions (e.g., *"What is your project management experience?"*), list a 
high-level summary of your key skills and immediately ask: *"Would you like me 
to dive deeper into any of those areas?"* [97-100]. This provides a menu of 
choices and ensures you only deliver the details they actually care about [98, 
100].
*   **Co-Creation**: Instead of treating strategic tech or business questions as
a quiz with one right answer, turn the interview into a live problem-solving 
session [101]. State your general approach to solving that type of problem, and 
then immediately ask clarifying questions about their current tools, what is 
broken, and what is causing friction [101-104]. This models how you would 
actually collaborate with stakeholders as a future co-worker [102, 104-106].
*   **BAMF Binder**: A step-by-step virtual interview strategy where you open 
work samples, reports, or projects on your desktop tabs before the call starts 
[107, 108]. When asked a highly technical or situational question, summarize 
your answer first, offer to share your screen, and walk them through your actual
work samples to make a stellar impression [108, 109].
