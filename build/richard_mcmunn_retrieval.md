Richard McMunn strongly rejects several popular pieces of interview advice, 
candidate mindsets, and application habits, calling them major mistakes that 
lead to immediate rejection:

### 1. Rejection of Popular Preparation and Mindset Advice
*   **"Just be yourself":** McMunn strongly disagrees with this common advice, 
calling it wrong and outdated [1]. He explains that a job interview is a formal 
professional assessment, not a casual meeting; therefore, you must actively sell
your value, project high standards, and demonstrate intense preparation rather 
than simply acting as you would in everyday life [1].
*   **"Don't dress professionally":** He dismisses the social media trend where 
figures advise candidates not to dress formally so the employer can see "the 
real you" [2, 3]. He calls this "complete rubbish" and bad advice, arguing that 
a smart, professional outfit immediately signals high standards, respect, and 
seriousness [1-4].
*   **"Being flexible makes you a slave":** He strongly disagrees with those who
warn candidates not to offer flexibility outside of their strict job description
because it makes them a "slave to the employer" [5]. He labels this "absolute 
rubbish," stating that demonstrating adaptability and a willingness to help the 
company in times of need is highly attractive to employers [5-7].

### 2. Disagreements with Common Interview Responses
*   **"It's on my CV or resume":** When asked to introduce yourself or walk 
through your experience, telling the interviewer "everything you need to know is
on my resume" is a critical mistake [8-11]. McMunn notes that this response 
makes you look slightly frustrated or lazy, and it is a guaranteed way to fail 
the interview [8, 11, 12].
*   **"I am a perfectionist" (or claiming to have no weaknesses):** McMunn 
strongly rejects the cliché of using perfectionism as a weakness, a tactic used 
by 70% of candidates [13-15]. He emphasizes that this response is viewed as 
dishonest or lacking in self-awareness, and warns that failing to provide a 
genuine, safe weakness with an improvement plan will result in rejection [13, 
16-19].
*   **"I would tell my manager about the conflict":** In response to conflict 
resolution questions, he strongly rejects saying you would immediately escalate 
the issue to a manager or supervisor [20-23]. Hiring managers want to employ 
people who are mature enough to take ownership of interpersonal issues and 
attempt to resolve them independently first [20, 22-24].
*   **"I just need a job" or "The pay is good":** He advises against showing 
desperation or admitting that your primary motivations are a paycheck, proximity
to your home, or simply "needing any job" [25-29]. These answers display a 
complete lack of genuine passion, commitment, and alignment with the company's 
values [25, 27-29].
*   **Admitting "I have no experience":** He strongly advises freshers or 
graduates against making excuses for their lack of professional experience [11].
Instead of stating you don't have experience, you should pivot to highlighting 
transferable academic skills, a high level of self-motivation, and your 
eagerness to be trained [30-33].

### 3. Rejection of Common Interview Closing Habits
*   **Saying "No, I don't have any questions":** Saying you have no questions 
because "everything was covered in the interview" is a massive missed 
opportunity [34-37]. He notes that this response baffles him because it implies 
you are prepared to spend roughly 25% of your life at a company without wanting 
to know anything deeper about its culture or challenges [35, 37].
*   **Asking about pay, benefits, holidays, or perks too early:** McMunn advises
against asking questions that focus on what you can *get* from the employer 
(like pay reviews, benefits, or upcoming holidays) rather than what you can 
*offer* [38-41]. Specifically, asking about planned time off at the end of an 
interview signals that you are already thinking of not being at work before you 
have even been hired [38, 41].

### 4. Rejection of Modern CV Drafting Mistakes
*   **Using Artificial Intelligence (AI) to write your CV:** He strongly warns 
against using Chat GPT to generate resumes or personal statements [42-44]. 
Employers are actively using advanced software to reject AI-written CVs at 
record rates because they make every candidate sound identical and lack 
concrete, personalized evidence [44-46].
*   **Using text boxes, tables, fancy fonts, or graphics:** While candidates use
these to make their CVs look visually appealing, McMunn rejects them entirely 
[47, 48]. Automated Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS) cannot scan text inside 
tables or text boxes, which ultimately makes the CV unreadable and leads to 
automated rejection [47-50].
*   **The "one-size-fits-all" CV approach:** He rejects using the same generic 
CV for multiple job applications [51, 52]. Failing to manually compare and 
mirror the exact keywords of each specific job description on your CV will cause
it to be instantly filtered out by the ATS software [48, 52-54].

the candidate’s passion with detailed research of the company’s reputation and 
values [14-17].
*   **Strengths and "Safe" Weaknesses:** Matching strengths to the job 
description [18-20] and presenting a genuine, non-cliché weakness (such as 
struggling to say no, impatience, or public speaking) with a corrective action 
plan is a highly frequent topic [21-24].
*   **Salary Expectations and Negotiations:** Successfully navigating pay 
discussions using researched market ranges to state an exact desired figure

investment [25-30].
*   **Asking Questions and Closing Statements:** McMunn repeatedly addresses the
critical error of asking no questions at the end of an interview [31-33]. He 
focuses on teaching candidates to ask 2 to 3 smart, company-focused questions

*   **Professional CV/Resume Writing:** Designing Applicant Tracking System

### 2. Question Types Handled Most Frequently

McMunn categorizes job interview questions into specific structural types,

*   **Opening/Introduction Questions:** Standardized prompts such as **"Tell me 
about yourself"** or **"Introduce yourself"** [45-48].
*   **Behavioral and Competency Questions:** Prompted by phrases like *"Tell me 
about a time when..."* or *"Describe a situation when..."* [49-52]. McMunn 
strictly mandates the **STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result)** to 
structure these answers [53-56], focusing on scenarios like:
    *   **Working as part of a team** [57, 58].
    *   **Dealing with conflict with a co-worker** [59-62].
    *   **Overcoming a difficult problem or challenge** [63-66].
    *   **Making a mistake or failing** [67-70].
    *   **Handling multiple tasks and tight deadlines** [71-74].
*   **Situational Questions:** Scenario-based questions that test hypothetical 
responses [75]. Examples include dealing with an **angry or difficult customer**
[76-78] or responding to an underperforming team member [79-81].
*   **Tricky or Curveball Questions:** Probing questions designed to test 
honesty and self-awareness, such as **explaining gaps in employment history** 
[82-85], discussing what you do in your free time [86-89], or describing 
something about yourself **not on your resume** [90-92].
