### **PPF (Present, Past, and Future) Framework**
This structured model is designed to craft the perfect answer to the standard 
opening question **"Tell me about yourself"** or **"Walk me through your 
resume"** [1-3]. Instead of reciting your entire life chronology [4], you divide
your response into three clear timeframes:
*   **Present (under 1 minute)**: A quick professional snapshot of your current 
role, the success metrics you are measured against on the job, and a recent 
high-impact achievement you are particularly proud of [5, 6].
*   **Past (under 1 minute)**: A highlight of a single previous work experience,
internship, or leadership role that is highly relevant to the position you are 
applying for, emphasizing the transferable skills you developed [5, 7, 8].
*   **Future (around 30 seconds)**: A forward-looking statement that logically 
connects your professional trajectory with the target role, detailing the value 
you can bring and why you fit the position [5, 9, 10].

---

### **CARL Method**
An advanced structural framework for answering **behavioral interview 
questions** (which usually start with prompts like "Tell me about a time 
when...") [11, 12]. It stands for:
*   **Context**: A concise blending of both the "Situation" and "Task" from the 
traditional STAR method to keep the background information highly targeted [13, 
14].
*   **Action**: The specific steps you personally took to handle the challenge 
[13, 15].
*   **Results**: The quantifiable outcomes of your actions [13, 15].
*   **Learnings**: The critical concluding section where you reflect on the 
experience, share what you took away from it, and explain how you have since 
applied that lesson to other situations [15-18]. Highlighting your learnings 
shows self-awareness and professional maturity [18, 19].

---

### **RCS Framework**
A foolproof step-by-step method for answering **situational (hypothetical) 
interview questions** (prompts starting with "Imagine if..." or "What would you 
do if...") [11, 20, 21]. It involves:
*   **Rephrase the question**: Repeat the interviewer's scenario back to them in
your own words to ensure complete alignment [21].
*   **Clarify the problem**: Ask targeted questions about the constraints of the
scenario (e.g., why a task is impossible, what resources are available, or how 
similar situations were handled in the past) [21, 22].
*   **Structure your thoughts**: Ask the interviewer for one minute to organize 
your ideas, which buys you time in a natural way and shows you are a thoughtful 
decision-maker who doesn't just blurt out the first thing that comes to mind 
[22, 23].

---

### **Specific to Broad Formula**
This mental model is designed to answer the difficult question **"Where do you 
see yourself in 5 years?"** [24, 25]. To give an authentic answer that signals 
you are a dependable, long-term employee without backing yourself into an

*   **Years 1–2 (Specific)**: Speak specifically about concrete, researched 
goals like keeping an open mind, absorbing industry knowledge, or applying your 
current skills to make your manager's life easier [29, 30].
*   **Years 3–5 (Broad)**: Pivot to broader goals as uncertainty increases, 
focusing on the overall direction of your career—such as specializing in a 
business vertical, building deeper client relationships, and eventually

### **Over-Relying on the STAR Method**
While the STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) method is widely taught as the 
gold standard for behavioral questions, Jeff Su strongly rejects adhering to it 
religiously `[1, 2]`. He points out two major limitations: it **allocates too 
much time to background information** instead of your achievements `[1]`, and it
**neglects takeaways and learnings** `[1]`. Su also warns that strictly 
following STAR makes candidates sound **robotic, like they are repeating 
memorized answers** `[3]`. He advocates for the **CARL** (Context, Action, 
Results, Learnings) method instead `[3]`.

---

### **Using a "Strength in Disguise" as a Weakness**
When answering "What is your biggest weakness?", Su strongly disagrees with the 
common advice to offer a **"weakness that's really a strength in disguise"** 
(such as claiming you are "too much of a perfectionist" or "work too hard") 
`[4]`. He rejects this as disingenuous `[4]`. However, he also cautions against 
the opposite extreme—sharing **"deal-breaker" weaknesses** like an inability to 
manage multiple projects, lack of attention to detail, or not being a people 
person, which instantly flag you as a high-maintenance liability `[4, 5]`. 
Instead, he recommends highlighting a **skill expected of someone more senior** 
`[5]`.

---

### **Walking Through Your Entire Life Story**
During the "Tell me about yourself" opening question, Su highlights **recounting
your entire chronological life story** as a massive mistake `[6]`. Candidates 
often ramble through where they grew up, what they majored in, and every minor 
job they've held `[6, 7]`. He rejects this approach because it fails to 
establish immediate professional credibility `[6, 7]`. Instead, he advises 
starting with your **most current, relevant experience and moving backward** 
using the Present, Past, Future (PPF) structure `[8, 9]`. 

---

### **Naming a Specific Title for the "5-Year Plan"**
When asked "Where do you see yourself in 5 years?", Su warns that **naming a 
specific job title or role is a major trap** `[10, 11]`. If you name a title 
that is too aggressive, the interviewer may view you as pretentious and 
arrogant; if it is too conservative, they will question your confidence and 
ability `[12]`. Instead of trying to guess a destination title, he argues your 
answer should focus on the **overall journey and the skills you hope to 
develop** using a "Specific to Broad" progression `[10, 12, 13]`.

---

### **Asking Easily Searchable Questions**
Su rejects the habit of asking generic questions at the end of an interview, 
calling **asking questions you can easily find the answer to online** the 
biggest mistake you can make `[14]`. Doing so signals a complete lack of 
preparation to the hiring manager `[14, 15]`. He also rejects the common "What 
is your day-to-day like?" question because most modern professionals **do not 
have a typical day-to-day** and will only give a vague description `[16]`. He 
advises asking tailored questions that *only* the specific interviewer sitting 
across from you can answer `[14, 15]`.

---

### **Mentioning Salary, Location, or Commute First**
When explaining "Why do you want to work here?", Su strongly advises candidates 
to **avoid mentioning salary, location, or commute** `[17]`. He warns that 
bringing up these factors **"sucks the energy out of the conversation"** because
they focus entirely on what the company can provide for you, rather than the 
selfless value you can bring to the table `[17, 18]`.

### **5. SCQA & The Pyramid Principle for PM Case Structuring**
Product Management and tech leadership roles heavily prioritize communication 
efficiency. The expert rejects standard chronological storytelling (*"here is 
what I did, here is what I found, here is my action"*), instead applying 
top-down frameworks [13]:
*   **The Pyramid Principle / BLUFF (Bottom Line Up Front)**: Lead with your 
final action-oriented recommendation first, then support it with three logical 
data-driven arguments, and restate your action at the end [14-18]. 
*   **SCQA (Situation, Complication, Question, Answer)**: Frame product 
challenges by establishing the current landscape (**Situation**), introducing 
the core friction or conflict (**Complication**), identifying the pivot 
(**Question**), and delivering your recommendation (**Answer**) [19-21]. 

Applying these frameworks prevents tech candidates from sounding like "dumb 
interns" who merely cram information together, transforming raw data into an 
executive-level narrative [22-24].
